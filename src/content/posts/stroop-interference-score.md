Finish a Stroop test and you get a handful of numbers. Most of them are what you'd expect: how many you got right, how fast you were. One of them is different. The interference score doesn't measure speed as such. It measures how much one particular thing, a colour word in the wrong ink, slowed you down.

It's a single subtraction, and it's the number the test was designed to produce. If you haven't taken the test yet, [do that first](/#test) so you have your own figure to look at while you read.

## What the number represents

A Stroop test mixes a few kinds of trial. On a **matching** (congruent) trial, the word and the ink agree: RED in red. On a **clashing** (incongruent) trial they don't: RED in blue. Many versions, ours included, add **neutral** trials, where an ordinary word like LAMP appears in a colour, so there's no colour word to get in the way.

For a fluent reader, reading isn't optional. The word gets processed whether you like it or not, and on a clashing trial it points to the wrong answer. You have to hold it back before you can name the ink. Your interference score is the cost of doing that, in milliseconds.

If you want to see why reading barges in like this, [the Stroop effect explained](/stroop-effect-explained/) goes through it in detail.

## How it's calculated

On this site, the calculation runs in three steps.

1. Take your correct answers on matching trials and find the **median** time. The median is the middle value once the times are sorted. We use it instead of the average because one slow answer, say you blinked or glanced away, barely moves it.
2. Do the same for your clashing trials.
3. Subtract the first number from the second.

![Diagram showing incongruent minus congruent reaction time](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Incongruent-minus-congruent.webp "The gap between the two trial types is your score. The numbers here are just an example.")

Say your matching median is 560 ms and your clashing median is 650 ms. Your interference is 90 ms. That's the extra time the clash took.

Two filters happen before any of this. Answers under 200 ms are thrown out because nobody can genuinely see a colour and decide that quickly, so they're guesses. Answers over 3 seconds are thrown out too, because at that point something other than the task was going on. Wrong answers don't count towards timing, though they do count against your accuracy.

### Other ways to calculate it

Not every test does it like this. Some use neutral trials as the baseline instead of matching ones, which gives you the cost of the clash without the small boost a matching word gives you. Our report shows that version too.

The paper-and-pencil Golden Stroop, which clinicians often use, works on a different principle entirely. People name as many items as they can in 45 seconds on three cards (words, colour patches, and clashing colour words), and the interference score compares the third card against a predicted total worked out from the first two. Same idea, different ruler, so you can't compare a Golden score with a millisecond score from a website.

## Why it ignores raw speed

Raw times are hard to interpret. Picture two people who both take 800 ms on clashing words. One of them is fast at everything and was badly thrown by the clash. The other is just a slow, careful presser who handled the clash fine. The 800 ms hides that difference completely.

Subtracting the matching time fixes this, because your matching time already contains your reading speed, your finger speed and the lag of your keyboard. Take it away and what's left is mostly the clash itself. That's why researchers usually report the difference rather than the raw time.

![Reaction time distribution with the median marked](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Reaction-time-distribution.webp "Response times bunch up with a long tail of slow answers, which is why the median is a safer summary than the average.")

There's a second benefit for online tests. Keyboards, touchscreens and screens add their own delays, often tens of milliseconds, and they differ from one device to the next. Because both trial types are measured on the same device, most of that delay is in both numbers and drops out when you subtract.

## What's a normal range?

There's no official cut-off, because the number depends on the version of the task, how many colours it uses and how people respond. Pressing keys gives smaller interference than saying colours out loud, for example. For keyboard and touch versions like ours, adults commonly land somewhere around 40 to 120 ms. Our report labels results in rough bands:

- **Under 40 ms**: very small
- **40 to 120 ms**: typical
- **120 to 200 ms**: larger than typical
- **200 ms or more**: large

Treat these as a rough guide, not a diagnosis. They aren't adjusted for age, and they aren't drawn from a formal normative sample.

### Age and the second number on your report

Interference does tend to grow in later life. But a well-known 1998 meta-analysis by Paul Verhaeghen and Liesbeth De Meersman found that most of that growth comes from general slowing. Older adults are a bit slower at almost everything, so every gap gets stretched, including this one.

That's why the report also shows **relative interference**: the gap as a percentage of your own matching speed. If you take 560 ms on matching words and 650 ms on clashing ones, your relative interference is about 16%. It changes much less with age than the raw gap, which makes it the fairer number for comparing yourself with someone thirty years younger or older. Our guide to [what counts as a good Stroop score](/good-stroop-test-score/) has more on reading results by age.

## When your score is high

Most of the time a high score has a boring explanation. Not enough sleep, a hangover, a noisy room, a laptop keyboard you're not used to, or just not having done the test before. Your first run is often the slowest.

If it's high again and again, under good conditions, that's more interesting but still not a diagnosis. Groups of people with ADHD, depression or a brain injury show somewhat more interference on average in research studies. The overlap with everyone else is huge, though. Plenty of people with those conditions score in the typical range, and plenty of people without them score high. If you're worried about your attention, the useful step is to talk to a doctor, and our piece on [whether an online test can diagnose ADHD](/online-adhd-attention-test/) explains why.

## Getting a number you can trust

One result is a snapshot. A handful taken under the same conditions tells you far more. A few habits help:

- Test at the same time of day, on the same device, somewhere quiet.
- Do the practice round first, so learning the keys isn't mixed into your score.
- Use the Standard level for tracking. Forty trials gives a steadier median than twenty.
- Don't retake it ten times in a row. You'll get faster at the game, which isn't the same as better attention.

The test saves your last few results in your browser, so you can watch the number over a few weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Stroop interference score?

It's the extra time you need to name the ink when the word names a different colour. On this site it's your median time on clashing trials minus your median time on matching trials, counting correct answers only.

### Why use the median instead of the average?

Response times have a long tail. A single slow answer, from a sneeze or a glance at your phone, can pull an average up a lot. The median barely moves, so it's a better summary of how you usually responded.

### What is a normal interference score?

On keyboard and touchscreen versions, many adults fall between about 40 and 120 ms. It depends on the test design, so compare results from the same test, and ideally compare with your own earlier results.

### Do mistakes affect the score?

Wrong answers are left out of the timing, so they don't change the interference number directly. They do lower your accuracy, which is shown separately. A fast score with lots of errors isn't a strong result.

### What is relative interference?

It's your interference as a percentage of your matching-word time. Because it takes general speed out of the picture, it's fairer for comparing people of different ages or on different devices.

### Can my interference score be negative?

Occasionally, on a short test. It usually means you had very few clashing trials or a couple of slow matching answers. Take the Standard test again and it will almost always come out positive.

### Does age affect interference?

The raw gap tends to grow with age, but mostly because everything gets a little slower. Relative interference changes much less, which is why we show both.

### Can I lower my score?

A bit. Good sleep, a quiet room and familiarity with the keys help straight away. Regular aerobic exercise has some evidence behind it for executive function in general. Expect small changes, and look at the trend over several sessions.
