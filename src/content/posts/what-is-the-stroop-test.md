Look at the word **RED** printed in blue ink and say the colour of the ink. Your mouth wants to say red. You know the answer is blue. For a split second the two fight, and you can feel it.

That tug-of-war is the whole idea behind the Stroop test, and psychologists have been studying it for about ninety years. At its core, the test checks how well you can pay attention to one thing while something else keeps grabbing at you. This guide covers where it came from, what it measures and how it works, so that when you [take the free Stroop test](/#test) you know exactly what your numbers mean.

## The test at a glance

The task is almost embarrassingly simple. You see a word printed in coloured ink, and you name the ink colour. Not the word. The ink.

When the word and the colour agree, people breeze through. The trouble starts when they disagree. Show someone RED in blue and watch them hesitate, correct themselves or just say the wrong thing.

That hesitation is called the **Stroop effect**, and the test exists to catch it and measure it.

![Colour words printed in mismatched ink colours](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Stroop-test-colour-and-word-cards.webp "The classic layout, where the word and the ink disagree.")

### Why does such an easy task trip us up?

Because you've read so much that reading runs on autopilot. Your brain reads the word whether you want it to or not, and then you have to push that reflex aside to give the slower, correct answer. How long the pushing takes, measured in thousandths of a second, says a surprising amount about how your attention behaves under pressure. For the full explanation, see [the Stroop effect explained](/stroop-effect-explained/).

## What is the purpose of the Stroop test?

The purpose of the Stroop test is to measure how well you can ignore an automatic response and follow a rule instead. Reading the word is automatic; naming the ink colour is the rule. The extra time you need when the two disagree shows how strong your inhibitory control and selective attention are at that moment.

That is why researchers and clinicians use it to look at attention, impulse control and processing speed, and to track how these change with age, tiredness, stress or some health conditions.

## A short history

![Illustration of old colour word cards](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Early-Stroop-test-cards-from-1935.webp "An illustration of the printed cards used in early versions of the task.")

The test is named after John Ridley Stroop, who was working on his doctorate at George Peabody College in Nashville when he ran the experiments. He published them in 1935 under the title *Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions*. In his key experiment, naming the inks of 100 clashing colour words took about 74% longer than naming 100 plain colour squares. The full paper is still free to read in the [Classics in the History of Psychology archive](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Stroop/) at York University.

Stroop wasn't the first person to notice that words and colours interfere, but he measured it properly and gave it a clear shape. The paper then sat fairly quietly until interest picked up in the 1960s. It's now one of the most cited studies in the history of experimental psychology. You can read more about the man in our article on [John Ridley Stroop](/john-ridley-stroop/).

## What it actually measures

A common misunderstanding is that the test is about colour vision or reading ability. It's neither. It targets a few connected mental skills that psychologists group under [executive function](/what-is-executive-function/).

### Selective attention

Your ability to focus on what matters and filter out the rest. In every clashing trial, the word is noise you're supposed to ignore. Answering with the ink means giving your attention to the colour and not letting the word run off with it.

### Processing speed

How quickly you take something in, work out what it is and respond. The test times every answer in milliseconds, so it picks up differences far too small to notice yourself.

![Brain diagram highlighting the prefrontal cortex](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Prefrontal-cortex-and-attention-control.webp "The front of the brain does much of the work of staying on task.")

### Inhibitory control

The brain's brake. Because reading is automatic, the pull to read the word is strong, and stopping yourself so you can name the ink is a direct test of that braking. That's why the task appears so often in research on attention and impulsiveness, including studies of [the Stroop test and ADHD](/stroop-test-and-adhd/).

## How a trial works: three conditions

A modern Stroop test mixes three kinds of trial, and the comparison between them is where the score comes from.

![Matching, clashing and neutral Stroop trials side by side](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Three-Stroop-trial-types-side-by-side.webp "Matching, clashing and neutral trials. Some tests use coloured shapes for neutral trials; ours uses ordinary words.")

### Matching (congruent) trials

The word and the ink agree, like GREEN in green. Both point at the same answer, so responses are quick and accurate. These give you a baseline.

### Clashing (incongruent) trials

The word and the ink disagree, like BLUE in red, and the answer is red. These are the hard ones. Your reading habit pushes you towards the wrong answer, and the time it takes to override it is the heart of what the test measures.

### Neutral trials

No clash at all. Depending on the test, you might see a row of Xs, coloured shapes, or an ordinary word like LAMP printed in colour. They show your plain colour-naming speed, which helps separate the cost of the clash from the general effort of naming a colour. Our test uses ordinary words, so every neutral trial still involves reading something.

## The Stroop test procedure

Whatever the version, the procedure follows the same pattern:

1. **Instructions.** You are told to name the ink colour and ignore the word.
2. **Practice.** A few trials let you learn the responses (spoken, or keys and buttons).
3. **Baseline trials.** You name colours without a clash: matching words, coloured patches or neutral words.
4. **Interference trials.** You name the ink of colour words printed in a different colour.
5. **Scoring.** The difference in speed (and errors) between the baseline and interference trials gives the Stroop interference score.

In computer versions like ours, the trial types are mixed in a random order and every response is timed. In paper versions, each condition is a separate card and you name as many items as you can in a fixed time.

## Versions you might come across

Over the years the task has been turned into several standard tests. Clinicians often use the **Golden Stroop** (Charles Golden, 1978), where you name as many items as you can in 45 seconds across three printed cards. The **Victoria Stroop** is a shorter version. The **Color-Word Interference Test** in the D-KEFS battery adds a switching condition. Each has its own scoring rules and norms, so their scores can't be compared directly with each other or with an online test.

Computer versions, like ours, show one word at a time and time each answer, which gives much finer detail about speed and mistakes.

## How the score works

Most reports give you three numbers that matter: your typical response time, your accuracy and your interference score. The interference score is the gap between your speed on clashing trials and on matching ones. A small gap suggests strong attention control. A large one means the clash really slowed you down.

![Flashcards with colour words and a stopwatch](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Stroop-Test-Flashcards-and-Stopwatch.webp "Before computers, the test was timed with printed cards and a stopwatch.")

Those numbers mean little in isolation. Response times get a bit slower with age, which is completely normal, and the device you use matters too. Our guide to [what counts as a good Stroop score](/good-stroop-test-score/) explains the usual ranges, and [understanding your interference score](/stroop-interference-score/) goes deeper into the main number.

One thing to be clear about. An online Stroop test is a fun and useful snapshot of your attention and speed, but it's not a medical assessment and it can't diagnose anything. If you're worried about your memory, attention or thinking, talk to a qualified health professional.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Stroop test in simple terms?

A quick timed task where you name the ink colour of a word and ignore what the word says. It shows how well you can focus and control your attention.

### Who created the Stroop test?

John Ridley Stroop, an American psychologist. He described it in a 1935 paper, *Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions*, based on his doctoral research at George Peabody College.

### What does the Stroop test measure?

Mainly selective attention, processing speed and inhibitory control. These all fall under what psychologists call executive function.

### How long does the Stroop test take?

Our Standard test is 40 trials and takes about two minutes, plus around thirty seconds for the optional practice round.

### Is the Stroop test the same as the Stroop effect?

No. The test is the task you do. The Stroop effect is the slowdown you experience when the word and the ink colour disagree.

### Can I take the Stroop test online for free?

### Is the Stroop test scientifically valid?

The effect is one of the most reliable in psychology, and standardised clinical versions have published norms. Online versions are good for learning and tracking yourself over time, but they aren't clinical tools.

### What is a good result on the Stroop test?

Usually high accuracy (90% or more) with a small to typical interference score, ideally consistent across a few sessions. Our good score guide explains the ranges in detail.
