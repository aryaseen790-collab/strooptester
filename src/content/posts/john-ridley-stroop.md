The Stroop effect is named after a man who wrote one famous paper on it and then, more or less, moved on. John Ridley Stroop published *Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions* in 1935. It came out of his doctoral research, and it showed something you can check for yourself in a couple of minutes: naming the ink colour of a word is much harder when the word spells a different colour.

You can feel that in the [free Stroop test](/#test). Here's the story of where it came from.

![Illustration of a 1930s psychology laboratory](/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/1930s-psychology-lab.webp "An illustration of the kind of laboratory where early colour-naming studies were run. It isn't a photograph of Stroop's lab.")

## Who was John Ridley Stroop?

Stroop was born in 1897 in Rutherford County, Tennessee, and died in 1973. He did his graduate work at George Peabody College for Teachers in Nashville, finishing his PhD there in 1933. The interference experiments were part of that work and were published two years later in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*.

What surprises most people is what he did next. Stroop spent most of his career teaching at David Lipscomb College, also in Nashville, where he taught psychology and later Bible. He was a preacher, and he wrote several books on religion. He didn't build a research career around his famous finding. The man whose name appears in thousands of later papers was mainly a teacher.

## Was he the first?

Not quite. In the 1880s James McKeen Cattell noticed that people name colours more slowly than they read words, and in 1929 the German psychologist Erich Jaensch had looked at a version of the clash between words and colours. What Stroop did was design clean experiments, time them carefully, and show how large and how one-sided the interference was. That's why the effect carries his name.

## The three experiments

The 1935 paper reported three linked experiments. The full text is still freely available in York University's [Classics in the History of Psychology archive](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Stroop/), and it's surprisingly readable.

### Experiment 1: reading the words

Seventy college students read aloud a sheet of 100 colour names. On one sheet the words were printed in black. On the other, each word was printed in a colour that didn't match it. The clashing ink barely mattered. Reading took about 43 seconds instead of 41, a difference too small to count as a real effect.

### Experiment 2: naming the ink

This was the important one. A hundred participants named the ink colours on two kinds of sheet. One had plain coloured squares. The other had colour words printed in clashing inks. Naming the squares took about 63 seconds on average. Naming the ink of the clashing words took about 110 seconds, roughly 74% longer.

So reading wasn't disturbed by colour, but colour naming was badly disturbed by reading. That lopsidedness is the heart of the Stroop effect.

### Experiment 3: what practice does

Stroop then gave people several days of practice naming the inks of clashing words. They got faster, but the improvement had limits, and something odd happened along the way. After all that practice, the words started to interfere a little with *reading*, the direction that had shown almost no effect before. Practice could shift the balance a bit, but reading stayed the stronger habit.

## What it showed

The key point is easy to say. For a fluent adult, reading is close to automatic. You can't switch it off on request. When a word and its ink disagree, the automatic reading response competes with the slower, deliberate job of naming the colour, and you pay for the competition in time and mistakes.

## How it became a classic

For a while the paper didn't get much attention. Interest grew from the 1960s, helped along by a 1966 review by Arthur Jensen and William Rohwer, and it has kept growing ever since. In 1991 Colin MacLeod reviewed [more than 700 studies of the effect](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.109.2.163), and a year later he called the task the "gold standard" of attentional measures. It's regularly listed among the most cited papers in experimental psychology.

Why did this one stick? A few reasons:

- **It needs almost nothing.** Some printed words and a stopwatch were enough in 1935.
- **The effect is big.** Nearly everyone who reads fluently shows it.
- **It's about something deep.** It captures the tug-of-war between habit and intention in one simple demonstration.

## From habits to brain scans

Stroop explained his results in the language of the 1930s, in terms of habits and learning. Later researchers added new ideas. Some focused on *automaticity*, the idea that skilled reading runs without effort. Others talked about *speed of processing*, the idea that the word simply arrives first. In 1990 Jonathan Cohen, Kevin Dunbar and James McClelland built a computer model in which the two skills are pathways of different strength competing for the same response, and that idea is still influential.

Brain imaging came later still. A 1990 PET study by José Pardo and colleagues linked the task to the anterior cingulate cortex, and later fMRI work tied it to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex too, two areas involved in noticing conflict and staying on task.

## The legacy

Today, versions of Stroop's task show up in research on attention, ageing, ADHD, depression, brain injury and more. There's an "emotional Stroop" that swaps colour words for emotionally loaded ones, and number and spatial versions too. The [American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/research-practice/conduct-research/stroop-effect) has written about how far the task has travelled since 1935.

Not bad for a dissertation by a man who went on to teach Bible in Nashville.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who was John Ridley Stroop?

An American psychologist, born in 1897 and died in 1973. He's remembered for his 1935 study of colour-word interference, which grew out of his doctoral work at George Peabody College in Nashville.

### When was the Stroop effect discovered?

Stroop published it in 1935, in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*. Earlier researchers had noticed related effects, but his paper is the one that defined it.

### What did the 1935 study find?

That naming the ink of a clashing colour word takes much longer than naming a plain colour patch (about 74% longer in his second experiment), while reading the word is hardly affected by the ink.

### Why is the study so famous?

It's simple, the effect is large and reliable, and it says something basic about how habits and intentions compete. MacLeod later called the task the "gold standard" of attentional measures.

### Did Stroop keep researching the effect?

Not really. He spent most of his career teaching at David Lipscomb College, first psychology and later Bible, and he wrote mainly about religion.

### Where was the study published?

In the *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, volume 18, issue 6, pages 643 to 662, under the title *Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions*.

### Can I read the original paper?

Yes. York University hosts the full text in its Classics in the History of Psychology archive, free to read online.
