If concentrating has felt harder than it should for a while, typing "ADHD test" into a search bar is a very normal thing to do. A few minutes later you have a score. It feels personal and immediate, and that's the appeal.

The short answer to the question in the title is no. An online test can't diagnose ADHD. But it isn't useless either, and the useful part is worth knowing about. This article covers what these tools do well, where they stop, and what an actual assessment involves.

> **Before we start:** everything on this site, including our [Stroop test](/#test), is educational. None of it can diagnose ADHD or any other condition. Only a qualified clinician can do that, after a proper assessment.

## Why a quiz can't settle it

Trouble concentrating has a long list of possible causes. Poor sleep is a big one. So are anxiety, depression, chronic stress, an underactive thyroid, low iron, some medicines, and simply having too much on. All of them can make focus slip for weeks or months at a time, and from the inside they can feel a lot like ADHD.

A quiz only sees your answers to its questions. It can't see your history, can't ask a follow-up question and can't rule any of those other causes out. That's the basic gap, and no amount of clever scoring closes it.

![Person taking an online ADHD attention test](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Online-ADHD-test-hero.webp "A quick online check can be a sensible first step. It's not the last one.")

## What an online test is good for

Used for the right job, these tools have real value:

- **Putting words to a vague feeling.** Ticking through a list of symptoms can help you notice patterns you'd never named.
- **Starting a conversation.** A completed screener gives you something concrete to take to an appointment.
- **A nudge.** For many adults, a result is what finally gets them to book the appointment they've been putting off for years.

What they can't do is tell you whether you have ADHD, separate it from the other causes of poor focus, or account for how your attention has worked across your whole life.

## Two kinds of online test

Online attention tools come in two broad types.

![A self-report questionnaire next to a reaction time task](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Screener-vs-task.webp "One kind asks how you behave. The other watches what you do.")

### Questionnaires

The most common type asks how often you experience certain things. The best-known example for adults is the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS), developed with the World Health Organization and published by Ronald Kessler and colleagues in 2005. It's short, it's been studied a lot, and it's a reasonable first filter.

It's still a self-report. It measures how you see yourself, and that can shift with your mood, with how much you've read about ADHD lately, and even with how a question is worded.

### Performance tasks

The second type asks you to *do* something. A reaction time task, a continuous performance test, or a colour-word task like the [Stroop test](/what-is-the-stroop-test/) records how you actually respond. These tap attention and response control directly, rather than relying on how you think you behave.

Neither type can diagnose anything. They do look at the question from different angles, though, which is why clinicians sometimes use both.

## How ADHD is actually diagnosed

ADHD is defined by criteria in the DSM-5, the diagnostic manual used by psychiatrists. In summary, as the [American Psychiatric Association explains](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/adhd/what-is-adhd), a diagnosis needs:

- at least six symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity in children, or five for people aged 17 and over
- symptoms that were present before age 12
- symptoms that have lasted at least six months
- symptoms that show up in two or more settings, such as home and work
- clear evidence that they interfere with daily life, and aren't better explained by something else

![Clinician talking with a patient during an ADHD assessment](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Clinical-evaluation.webp "An assessment brings together history, interviews and rating scales.")

Checking all of that takes judgement. An assessment is usually done by a psychiatrist, psychologist, or a trained GP or primary care doctor, and it normally includes:

- an interview about your current difficulties and your childhood
- standardised rating scales, often filled in by you and by someone who has known you a long time, like a parent or partner
- a medical and mental health history, to look for other explanations

There's no blood test or brain scan for ADHD. The [CDC's page on diagnosis](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/diagnosis/index.html) says the same. That's also why other conditions get sorted out at this stage. Problems with planning and focus can come from weak [executive function](/what-is-executive-function/) for all sorts of reasons, and telling them apart is exactly what a quiz can't do.

## Where attention tasks fit in

A timed task can add a second kind of information to a questionnaire. Research on the Stroop task shows how careful you have to be with it, though.

Studies of adults with ADHD often find slower and more variable responses, and more mistakes on clashing trials. On average, as a group. When Saskia van Mourik and colleagues pooled the child and adolescent studies in a 2005 meta-analysis, they found that the standard Stroop interference measure did not reliably separate children with ADHD from children without it. A later meta-analysis by Marjolein Lansbergen and colleagues (2007) found a difference, but it shrank considerably once general slowness was taken into account.

![Chart showing overlap between two groups' Stroop scores](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Group-overlap-chart.webp "An illustration of overlapping groups. Averages can differ while most individual scores sit in the same range.")

The point isn't that the task is worthless. It's that a group difference on average says very little about any one person. Your own score can't stand in for a clinical decision. For more on this research, see [the Stroop test and ADHD](/stroop-test-and-adhd/).

## Reading your result without overreading it

A high score on a screener means one sensible thing: it might be worth talking to a professional. It doesn't mean you have ADHD. A low score doesn't rule it out either.

![Reminder that a screening result is not a diagnosis](/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Not-a-diagnosis-note.webp "Treat a score as a reason to have a conversation, not as a label.")

The same goes for timed tasks. Before you read much into a Stroop result, it helps to know [what a typical score looks like](/good-stroop-test-score/). A few habits keep any result honest:

- Take tests when you're reasonably rested, not at the end of a draining day.
- Repeat a timed task on a couple of different days. One run can be thrown by a single distraction.
- Write down real examples from your life: missed deadlines, lost keys, conversations you drifted out of. A clinician will find these far more useful than a number.

## If you're worried

If focus, organisation or restlessness are causing problems at work, in your studies or at home, book an appointment with your doctor or a mental health professional. Bring your notes and any screener results. They can look at the whole picture, think about other causes and help you work out what would actually help.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an online test diagnose ADHD?

No. It can suggest whether an assessment might be worthwhile, but a diagnosis needs a clinician to review your history, rule out other causes and apply formal criteria.

### Are online ADHD tests accurate?

Well-designed screeners like the ASRS are reasonably good at picking out people who should probably be assessed. They aren't accurate in the sense of confirming a condition, and they were never meant to be.

### What's the difference between screening and diagnosis?

A screen is a quick filter that estimates whether further assessment is a good idea. A diagnosis is a clinical conclusion based on interviews, history, rating scales and ruling out other explanations. One takes minutes. The other takes a trained person and time.

### Should I trust my online result?

Treat it as a signal, not a verdict. A high result is a reason to talk to someone. A low one doesn't rule anything out, because attention changes with sleep, stress and setting.

### What happens in a proper ADHD assessment?

Usually an interview about your current and childhood symptoms, rating scales completed by you and someone who knows you well, and a medical and mental health history. There's no single physical test.

### Can a cognitive test help with an ADHD assessment?

It can add information. Some clinicians use attention and response control tasks as one piece of evidence among several, but never as the deciding piece.

### Are self-report ADHD quizzes reliable?

Validated scales are reliable enough to use as screeners and are widely used in research and clinics. They still rely on how you see yourself, so they support an assessment rather than replace it.

### What should I do after a high result?

Note the specific ways attention affects your day, then make an appointment with your doctor or a mental health professional and bring those notes with you.
