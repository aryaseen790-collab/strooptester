Try something. Say the word "green" out loud. No problem.

Now imagine the word GREEN printed in bright red ink, and your job is to say the colour of the ink. Your tongue catches. Part of you wants to say green. The right answer is red. For a moment the two answers push against each other before one wins.

That push is the Stroop effect. It's the small delay, and the occasional mistake, that happens when your brain has to ignore something it normally does without thinking. In this case, the thing it can't help doing is reading.

![Colour words printed in mismatched ink colours](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Stroop-Test-explained.webp "The classic set-up: colour words in the wrong ink.")

## What the Stroop effect is

The easiest way to see it is with three kinds of word.

1. **Matching:** the word and the ink agree, like BLUE in blue.
2. **Clashing:** they disagree, like BLUE in yellow.
3. **Neutral:** a word that has nothing to do with colour, like LAMP in yellow.

Ask someone to name the ink colours, and the clashing words slow them down every time. They're a bit slower and make a few more mistakes. Matching words are usually the fastest of all, because reading actually helps. The gap between matching and clashing words is the effect.

It's named after John Ridley Stroop, who described it in 1935 while working on his doctorate at George Peabody College in Nashville. His original paper is free to read in York University's [Classics in the History of Psychology archive](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Stroop/). It holds up well for something written nearly a century ago. We tell [his story here](/john-ridley-stroop/).

![Vintage illustration of research papers and a bar chart on a desk](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Where-the-Stroop-effect-was-first-described.webp "An illustration of early research. Stroop published his findings in 1935.")

One distinction that trips people up: the **Stroop effect** is the thing that happens in your head. The **Stroop test** is the timed task built to measure it. The effect is the thing. The test is the ruler. If you want the ruler, read [what the Stroop test is](/what-is-the-stroop-test/).

## Why reading gets in the way

You've read so many words in your life that reading happens on its own. You don't choose to read a shop sign any more than you choose to recognise a friend's face across a room. It just fires.

Naming a colour is different. It isn't automatic in the same way. You have to look, decide and then say or press something, and that takes a beat. So a fast, pushy process (reading) runs into a slower, more effortful one (naming the colour). Reading tends to arrive first. Your brain has to catch the wrong answer before it escapes and swap in the right one. The catching and the swapping are where your milliseconds go.

### Why practice makes reading so strong

Think about how much you read in a day: road signs, phone screens, labels, captions, messages. By adulthood you've read words millions of times, and that much repetition turns a skill into a reflex you can't switch off. Nobody drills colour naming like that, so it stays comparatively slow.

That imbalance is why the effect is so dependable. It shows up in nearly everyone who reads fluently, in language after language. It's one of the most replicated findings in psychology.

## Why the clash costs time

Picture two trains heading for the same junction. One is the reading train: fast, heavy, not much in the way of brakes. The other is the colour-naming train: slower, lighter, easier to stop. They both want the track at the same moment. Something has to give way, and working out which one takes a little time. That's the lag you feel.

There's an influential computer model of exactly this idea. In 1990 Jonathan Cohen, Kevin Dunbar and James McClelland described reading and colour naming as two pathways of different strength competing for the same response. Attention, in their model, boosts the weaker pathway just enough to let it win, and the boost takes time.

This is also why the effect makes such a tidy measure of focus. The harder it is for you to push the word aside, the bigger the gap. A small, steady gap means your attention control is doing its job. ## What's going on in the brain

![Brain diagram with a fast reading pathway and a slower colour-naming pathway](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Two-pathways-racing-in-your-brain.webp "The fast reading pathway tends to get there first.")

When the word and the ink disagree, two regions near the front of the brain do most of the work.

The first is the **anterior cingulate cortex**. Think of it as a smoke alarm for mental conflict. It doesn't put the fire out. It notices that two answers are competing and raises the alarm. A 1990 PET study by José Pardo and colleagues was one of the first to show this region becoming more active during clashing Stroop trials.

The second is the **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**. It responds to the alarm by keeping your attention on the rule you've been given, name the ink, and away from the tempting word. In 2000, Angus MacDonald and colleagues used fMRI to show these two jobs happening in different places: one region keeping the goal in mind, the other flagging the conflict.

All of this falls under what psychologists call [executive function](/what-is-executive-function/), the set of skills you use to stay on task and ignore distractions. The Stroop effect gives researchers a simple window into how the brain settles its own arguments.

## Why researchers love it

Few tasks in psychology are as well loved as this one, and the reason is practical. It costs almost nothing, takes a few minutes and works on almost anyone. You don't need a scanner or a lab to see it, and yet it says something real about attention and self-control.

So it has travelled a long way from Stroop's lab. Clinicians use standardised versions in assessments. Researchers use it to study ageing, sleep loss, stress and conditions like [ADHD](/stroop-test-and-adhd/). There's an "emotional Stroop" that swaps colour words for emotionally loaded ones, which has been used in research on anxiety and depression. There are versions with numbers and with arrows pointing the wrong way.

## Where you meet it in everyday life

In ordinary life the lag is too quick to notice. But the same kind of clash is everywhere: a "push" sign on a door with a handle built for pulling, a left turn signposted on the right, someone saying "up" while pointing down. Each time, a strong habit and the task in front of you disagree, and you slow down slightly while your brain sorts it out.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Stroop effect in simple terms?

It's the stumble you feel when you try to say the colour of a word and the word spells a different colour. Your brain reads first, out of habit, so naming the colour means overriding what it has already read.

### Why does the Stroop effect happen?

Reading is fast and automatic. Naming a colour is slower and takes effort. When the two disagree, your brain has to hold back the reading response and settle which answer wins, and that takes extra time.

### Who discovered the Stroop effect?

John Ridley Stroop, an American psychologist, who published it in 1935 based on his doctoral research at George Peabody College in Nashville.

### Is the Stroop effect the same as the Stroop test?

Not quite. The effect is the slowdown itself. The test is the timed task designed to measure it.

### Which parts of the brain are involved?

Mainly two areas at the front of the brain. The anterior cingulate cortex detects the conflict, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex keeps your attention on the task rule so you answer with the ink.

### Can practice reduce the Stroop effect?

A little. People get somewhat faster with practice, as Stroop himself found in 1935, but the effect rarely disappears, because reading stays automatic.

### Why is the Stroop effect important in psychology?

It's cheap, quick and works on almost everyone, which makes it a reliable way to study attention, habits and self-control. That's why it turns up in clinics and labs all over the world.

### Does everyone experience the Stroop effect?

Almost everyone who reads fluently does, in many languages and at most ages. The size of the effect is what varies between people.
