You've taken the test, the results are on screen, and you want to know if they're any good. Fair question. The honest answer is that there isn't one magic number. What counts as good depends on which figure you're looking at, how old you are, and which version of the test you took.

This guide goes through each number on your report, what's typical, and how to compare yourself fairly. If you haven't done the test yet, [take it first](/#test). The rest will make more sense with your own results in front of you. For background on the task itself, start with [what the Stroop test is](/what-is-the-stroop-test/).

## Your result is several numbers, not one

Our report gives you a few figures:

- **Interference:** how much slower you were on clashing words (RED in blue) than on matching ones (RED in red).
- **Relative interference:** that gap as a percentage of your matching-word time.
- **Accuracy:** the share of trials you got right.
- **Typical response:** the median time of your correct answers.
- **Speed + accuracy:** a combined score (inverse efficiency) that stops guessing from looking good.

They're separate because they can point in different directions. One person flies through and gets a quarter of the answers wrong. Another goes carefully and gets nearly everything right. Their times might look similar, but they did very different tests.

![Chart of accuracy against reaction time with four zones](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Speed-and-Accuracy-Balance.webp "The best results are both quick and accurate.")

### Why one number on its own can mislead

Psychologists call this the speed-accuracy trade-off. Almost anyone can go faster by being sloppier, or be more accurate by going slower. A sensible reading of your results weighs both. The strongest results don't give up one for the other.

## Average Stroop test results

People usually want a number to compare against, so here are the averages you can expect on a computer or phone version like ours. They describe healthy adults paying attention, on a keyboard or touchscreen, with four colours.

| Measure | Typical adult range | What pulls it up or down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | 90 to 98% | Rushing, distractions, a cramped phone screen |
| Typical response on matching words | about 450 to 650 ms | Age, device lag, how awake you are |
| Typical response on clashing words | about 520 to 750 ms | The same, plus how strongly the word pulls you |
| Interference (clashing minus matching) | about 40 to 120 ms | Tiredness, practice, age |
| Average time per word, including the pause | about 1.2 to 1.5 seconds | Mostly fixed by the test's timing |
| Time to finish the 40-word Standard test | about 1.5 to 2 minutes | Add 30 seconds for the practice round |

These are ranges, not a pass mark. They come from the spread of results commonly reported for keyboard and touch versions of the task, not from a formal normative study.

Paper versions are scored differently. In the Golden Stroop, which clinicians often use, people name as many items as they can in 45 seconds on each of three cards, and the raw counts are converted to T-scores adjusted for age and education. A T-score of 50 is average, and most people land between 40 and 60. Some online tests also roll speed and accuracy into a single "performance index". Ours reports inverse efficiency for the same purpose: your median correct time divided by the proportion you got right, so lower is better.

## Accuracy

Accuracy is the simplest number, so people often skip it. They shouldn't. When a healthy adult is paying attention, it's high: usually over 90%, often in the high 90s. Most slips happen on clashing words, where the word briefly wins the race. A couple of those is completely normal.

If you're down in the low 80s or below, think about why. Were you rushing? Did a notification pull your eyes away? Were you squinting at a phone in a dim room? One rough run doesn't mean much. A pattern across several careful runs is more worth noticing.

Also check how many answers the report says it left out. Anything faster than 200 ms counts as a guess, and on the Hard level anything slower than 1.5 seconds counts as a miss.

## Response time

Response times on a Stroop test are slower than people expect. Reacting to a single light can take around a quarter of a second for a young adult. In a Stroop trial you have to see the word, identify its colour, ignore what it says and pick one of four keys. That usually takes somewhere between about half a second and eight-tenths of a second, depending on the person and the device.

Age has its clearest effect here. Speed tends to peak in your twenties and then slow gradually, more noticeably after about fifty. Research on simple reaction time, such as a 2015 study by David Woods and colleagues, suggests part of that slowing is in movement and response rather than in noticing the stimulus.

The device matters too. Keyboards, touchscreens and displays all add their own delays, often 10 to 50 ms, and they vary from one machine to the next. So don't compare your raw time with figures from a lab study or from a friend's phone. Compare it with your own earlier results on the same device.

## Interference: the main number

![Diagram of the interference cost between matching and clashing trials](/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/What-Interference-Measures.webp "Interference is the extra time the clashing trials cost you.")

The interference score is the reason the Stroop test exists. It's the gap between your speed on clashing and matching trials, in other words the price you paid for the clash. Because your matching-word speed is subtracted out, your general speed (and most of your device's lag) drops away, leaving something closer to a pure measure of how well you held the word back. Our guide to [the interference score](/stroop-interference-score/) goes through the arithmetic.

What's typical depends heavily on the version of the test. Saying colours out loud gives bigger interference than pressing keys, and more colour options make it bigger again. For keyboard and touch versions like ours, adults commonly land somewhere around 40 to 120 ms. The report uses rough bands:

| Interference | Label |
| --- | --- |
| Under 40 ms | Very small |
| 40 to 120 ms | Typical |
| 120 to 200 ms | Larger than typical |
| 200 ms or more | Large |

These are guides, not clinical norms. A large gap isn't a warning sign in itself. It's common when you're tired, new to the keys or distracted.

### How interference changes over a lifetime

Children show very large interference at first, because reading isn't fully automatic yet and the competition is messy. It drops through the school years as reading becomes fluent, then stays fairly flat through adulthood.

In later life the raw gap usually grows again. But a 1998 meta-analysis by Paul Verhaeghen and Liesbeth De Meersman found that most of the age difference in Stroop interference is explained by general slowing. Older adults are a little slower at everything, so every gap gets stretched.

That's where **relative interference** comes in. It expresses the gap as a percentage of your own matching speed, so it changes much less with age. If you want to compare yourself with someone decades older or younger, it's the better number to use.

## Why we don't give you a percentile

You might have seen other Stroop sites (including older versions of this one) tell you that you're in "the 72nd percentile". We stopped doing that. A genuine percentile needs a large, carefully collected reference sample, taken on the same test, with known ages and devices. We don't have one, and most websites that quote percentiles don't either. Putting a precise-looking number on a rough estimate would be misleading.

So the report gives you plain-language bands and, more usefully, a history of your own results. Your own earlier scores, on the same device and under similar conditions, are the fairest comparison there is.

## So what counts as good?

Putting it together, a good Stroop result usually looks like this:

- accuracy of 90% or more
- interference in the very small or typical band
- similar results across a few separate sessions

There's no universal pass mark, and chasing one isn't worth it. A perfectly healthy 65-year-old will usually have slower times and a bigger raw gap than a 20-year-old, and that's fine.

If your result was lower than you hoped, it often responds to ordinary things. Sleep has the biggest effect. Regular aerobic exercise helps processing speed and executive function over time. And setup matters more than people think: shut out distractions, do the practice round and use a real keyboard if you can.

One more thing to be clear about: this test looks at attention and processing speed. It isn't a medical assessment, and a low score doesn't diagnose anything. If you're genuinely worried about your memory, focus or thinking, talk to a qualified clinician.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good Stroop test score?

High accuracy (90% or more) together with interference in the very small or typical range, and results that hold up across a few sessions. There's no single pass mark, because it depends on the test version, your age and your device.

### What is the average Stroop interference score?

It depends on the design. For keyboard and touch tests like ours, adults commonly fall somewhere around 40 to 120 ms. Spoken versions and versions with more colours usually show larger effects.

### How does age affect my score?

Response times slow gradually from early adulthood, and the raw interference gap usually grows with them. Most of that is general slowing, so relative interference, which takes your speed into account, changes much less.

### Is accuracy or speed more important?

Neither on its own. A fast time with lots of mistakes isn't a strong result, and perfect accuracy at a crawl isn't either. That's why the report shows both and combines them in an inverse efficiency score.

### What is an exceptional Stroop score?

Very high accuracy, fast responses and interference in the very small band, all at once. Practised and well-rested people sometimes get there. Don't worry if you don't.

### Why don't you show percentiles?

Because honest percentiles need a large reference sample collected on exactly this test, and we don't have one. Rough bands and your own history are more truthful.

### Can my score change from day to day?

Yes, and it's normal. Sleep, stress, caffeine, time of day, your device and the room you're in all move it. That's why a trend across several sessions tells you more than one result.

### What if my score is below average?

Check the obvious things first: tiredness, distraction, the device. Then try again on another day. A low score isn't a diagnosis. If you have ongoing concerns about your thinking, speak to a health professional.
