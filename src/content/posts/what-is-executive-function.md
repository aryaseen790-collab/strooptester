You're halfway through an email when your phone buzzes, the kettle starts to whistle and your kid wanders in asking where the scissors went. Somehow you finish the sentence. You ignore the phone, park the kettle in the back of your mind and say "kitchen drawer" without looking up.

You barely noticed doing it. That bit of juggling was your executive function.

In plain terms, executive function is the set of mental skills you use to plan, stay focused, keep things in mind and stop yourself from doing the first thing that pops into your head. It doesn't store memories or recognise faces. It manages. It decides what gets your attention and what gets ignored.

![Illustration of a brain hub linking working memory, inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility](/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Executive-Function-Hub.webp "Executive function acts as a manager for the rest of your thinking.")

## A working definition

The [Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/inbriefs/inbrief-executive-function/) describes executive function as the mental processes that let us plan, focus attention, remember instructions and juggle several tasks successfully. It's a useful definition because it points at things you actually do every day.

One thing people often miss: executive function isn't the same as intelligence. You can know a great deal and still struggle to start a task, stick with it or change course when the plan falls apart. Plenty of brilliant people lose their keys twice a day.

These skills also aren't fixed. Yours shift with sleep, stress, mood and even how hungry you are. Everyone has better and worse days.

## The three core skills

Most researchers follow a model set out by Akira Miyake and colleagues in 2000 and summarised in an influential review by Adele Diamond in the [Annual Review of Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143750) in 2013. It splits executive function into three core skills. More complicated abilities, like planning and reasoning, are built on top of them.

![Three cards showing working memory, inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility](/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Three-Core-Skills.webp "The three building blocks most researchers agree on.")

### Working memory

Working memory is a mental sticky note. It holds a small amount of information for a short time so you can use it: doing a sum in your head, following a recipe without rereading every line, remembering the start of this sentence by the time you reach the end. It's small and it fades fast. That's why you can walk into a room and forget why you came.

### Inhibitory control

This is the brake. Inhibitory control lets you hold back an automatic or tempting response so you can do something better. Not saying the rude thing. Not eating the whole packet. Not reading the word GREEN when it's printed in red and you've been asked for the ink colour.

That last example is the [Stroop effect](/stroop-effect-explained/), and it's one of the clearest ways to see inhibitory control at work. The extra time you need on a clashing word is, roughly, the time it takes your brake to grip.

### Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility is the gear shift. It lets you switch between tasks, change approach when something isn't working, and look at a problem from a different angle. Children who can play the same card game by two different sets of rules are using it. So are you, every time a meeting agenda gets thrown out at the last minute and you have to rethink on the spot.

## Where it happens in the brain

If executive function has a home, it's the prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain just behind your forehead. Brain imaging and studies of people with injuries to this area link it to stopping automatic responses, handling conflict, switching tasks, keeping goals in mind and planning ahead. That's more or less the list above.

![Side view of a brain with the prefrontal cortex highlighted](/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Prefrontal-Cortex-Highlight.webp "The prefrontal cortex sits at the front of the brain, behind the forehead.")

It doesn't work alone, though. It's in constant contact with other regions: the anterior cingulate cortex, which notices when something is going wrong, the parietal lobes, which help direct attention, and the basal ganglia deeper in the brain, which are involved in habits and starting and stopping actions. Think of a newsroom rather than a single editor.

This also explains why executive function is so easily knocked. The prefrontal cortex is one of the last areas of the brain to mature and one of the first to suffer from poor sleep, stress or a couple of drinks.

## Why it matters day to day

Executive function shows up almost everywhere. A student understands the material but can't organise an essay. A talented colleague keeps missing deadlines because every task feels like a wall. A driver knows the rules but reacts a moment too late when a cyclist swerves. These aren't knowledge problems. They're control problems.

![Collage of studying, working, driving and family moments](/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Everyday-Tasks-Collage.webp "Study, work, driving and family life all lean on the same skills.")

There's good evidence that these skills matter over the long run. A large study that followed about 1,000 children in Dunedin, New Zealand, from birth into their thirties (Moffitt and colleagues, 2011) found that self-control in childhood predicted adult health, income and run-ins with the law, even after allowing for intelligence and family background. Other research suggests executive function measures can predict early school success about as well as IQ does.

It's in relationships too. Pausing before you snap, remembering that your partner has a doctor's appointment, keeping calm when a toddler tests every limit. When executive function is running well, life feels smoother. When it's low, small jobs feel like hard work.

## How it develops and changes

Babies aren't born with executive function, only with the potential for it. Toddlers are famously bad at waiting, sharing and not throwing the spoon, because the brakes aren't built yet. The skills grow through childhood and keep developing into the mid-twenties. That late finish explains a lot about teenagers, who can be thoughtful one minute and wildly impulsive the next.

![Curve showing executive function rising in childhood and easing in later life](/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Development-Across-the-Lifespan.webp "A rough picture of the lifespan trend, not a precise chart.")

After that, executive function tends to hold fairly steady through middle age and then ease off gradually. Tasks take a little longer, and doing two things at once gets harder. This is normal ageing, not disease, and a lot of it goes along with general slowing in how quickly the brain takes in and acts on information.

The decline is usually gentle, and everyday habits make a difference. Sleep, exercise, social contact and staying mentally busy all help.

## How it's measured

You can't read executive function off a brain scan. Instead, psychologists use tasks that make the control system work hard and then watch how people cope.

The Stroop test is the classic example. Naming the ink colour of a clashing word sets your reading habit against your goal, and the delay that causes, the interference effect, is a direct measure of inhibitory control. Other tasks target the other skills: digit span for working memory, for instance, or card-sorting and task-switching tests for flexibility.

## Can you strengthen it?

To some extent. The gains are real but tend to be specific, and the boring habits do most of the work:

- **Sleep.** A tired prefrontal cortex is a sloppy one.
- **Exercise.** Regular aerobic exercise is one of the better-supported ways to help executive function in healthy adults, according to a 2013 review by Hayley Guiney and Liana Machado.
- **One thing at a time.** Constant task-switching makes you more distractible. Protect blocks of focused time.
- **Lists and reminders.** Writing things down takes the load off working memory and leaves your control system free for the hard parts.

What about brain-training apps? A large study published in *Nature* in 2010, run by Adrian Owen and colleagues with more than 11,000 participants, found that six weeks of online training made people better at the training games but not noticeably better at anything else. Most of these apps make you good at the app.

## A note on scores

This article is for general education, not medical advice. No online test, the Stroop test included, can tell you whether you have ADHD, dementia or anything else. If you're worried about your attention, memory or thinking, talk to a qualified health professional who can look at the whole picture.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is executive function?

The group of mental skills you use to plan, focus, keep information in mind and control impulses. It works like a manager, deciding what gets your attention and what doesn't.

### What are the three core executive functions?

Working memory (holding information briefly so you can use it), inhibitory control (holding back automatic responses) and cognitive flexibility (switching tasks or changing approach). More complex skills like planning are built on these.

### Where is executive function in the brain?

Mainly in the prefrontal cortex, behind the forehead, working together with the anterior cingulate cortex, the parietal lobes and the basal ganglia.

### Is executive function the same as IQ?

No. They're related, but you can have a high IQ and still struggle to start tasks, stay on them or switch when plans change. Some studies find executive function predicts real-world outcomes as well as IQ does, or better.

### How does executive function develop?

Slowly. It's almost absent in babies, grows through childhood and keeps maturing into the mid-twenties. It usually holds steady through middle age, then eases off gradually in later life.

### Can executive function be trained?

Partly. Sleep, regular exercise, protecting focused time and using lists all help. Brain-training games mostly improve performance on the games themselves.

### How is executive function measured?

With tasks that put the control system under pressure, like the Stroop test for inhibitory control, digit span for working memory and task-switching tests for flexibility.

### What weakens executive function?

Short term: poor sleep, stress, alcohol and hunger. Longer term, conditions like ADHD, depression or a head injury can affect it. The everyday dips usually recover with rest.
