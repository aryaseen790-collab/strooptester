You're in a café. Somebody at the next table is telling a long story about their landlord, the coffee machine is hissing, and there's music on. You still follow what your friend across the table is saying. That is selective attention: picking one stream of information and letting the rest fade into the background.

It sounds simple, but it isn't free. The brain has to decide, all the time, what deserves processing and what doesn't. When it gets this wrong you miss things, and when the distraction is strong enough you slow down. You can feel that slowdown yourself in our [free Stroop test](/#test), which is built around exactly this problem.

## What selective attention is

In psychology, selective attention means focusing on one source of information while ignoring others that are present at the same time. It's one part of a wider set of control skills that psychologists call [executive function](/what-is-executive-function/), and it leans heavily on one of them in particular, the ability to hold back an automatic response.

Two things are worth separating. There's what you attend to (a voice, a word, a corner of a screen) and there's what you are actively ignoring. Most of the interesting research is about the second part. Ignored information doesn't simply disappear, and that turns out to matter a lot.

## Everyday examples of selective attention

Most examples are boring, which is the point. It runs all day without you noticing it.

- Reading a book on a train while announcements play overhead.
- Picking out your child's voice in a noisy playground.
- Keeping your eyes on the car in front while billboards and phones compete for a glance.
- Finding a friend's face in a crowd at an airport.

The classic lab version is the "cocktail party" setting. In 1953 Colin Cherry played two different spoken messages to people at once, one in each ear, and asked them to repeat one message out loud as they heard it ([Cherry, 1953](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1907229)). Afterwards they could say almost nothing about the other ear. They noticed if the voice changed from male to female, but not when the language changed or the speech was played backwards.

## How selective attention works in the brain

Early theories pictured a filter. Donald Broadbent suggested in 1958 that the brain blocks unattended input very early, based on simple features like pitch or location, so only one channel reaches meaning. It was a neat idea, and it was wrong in an important way.

Neville Moray found that about a third of people noticed their own name when it turned up in the ear they were told to ignore ([Moray, 1959](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470215908416289)). Anne Treisman then showed that words in the ignored ear could pull attention across if they fitted the meaning of the attended message ([Treisman, 1960](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470216008416732)). So the ignored channel wasn't switched off. It was turned down, like a radio at low volume, and important or relevant things could still get through. This is usually called the attenuation model.

Later work looked at vision. Michael Posner described attention as something like a spotlight that you can move to a location before your eyes move there, which speeds up whatever appears in it. Brain imaging since then points to a network that includes the prefrontal cortex and parts of the parietal lobe, which bias processing towards what matters for your current goal. Robert Desimone and John Duncan called this "biased competition": everything in view competes for processing, and your goals tip the contest ([Desimone and Duncan, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ne.18.030195.001205)).

## The limits of selective attention

Here's where it gets uncomfortable. If attention is busy, you can miss things that are right in front of you. In the well known "gorilla" study, people counted basketball passes in a short video. Around half of them never noticed a person in a gorilla suit walking through the middle of the scene ([Simons and Chabris, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1068/p281059)). The effect has been repeated many times.

The opposite failure happens too. Sometimes a distraction gets through even though you're trying to ignore it, because it's processed automatically. Reading is the best example in adults. You can't look at a word and not read it. That's why naming the ink colour of the word RED printed in blue takes longer than naming a plain colour patch, the [Stroop effect](/stroop-effect-explained/). The word gets in before you can stop it, and you lose a few tenths of a second sorting it out.

Tiredness, stress, alcohol and simply doing two things at once all make filtering worse. I'd add phones to that list, mostly because they are designed to win the competition for attention.

## How selective attention is measured

There's no single test, but a few tasks come up again and again in research.

The Stroop task is probably the most used. You name the ink colour and ignore the word, and the extra time on clashing words (the interference score) shows how much the ignored information still affected you. Our guide to [what the Stroop test is](/what-is-the-stroop-test/) explains the trial types in more detail. Dichotic listening tasks, like Cherry's, test hearing. Visual search tasks ask you to find a target among distractors and time how long it takes as the number of distractors grows. Flanker tasks show an arrow surrounded by other arrows pointing the same or the opposite way.

None of these is a diagnosis of anything on its own. They give you a number for one narrow skill on one particular day. That number is useful mostly when you compare it with your own results over time.

## Can you improve selective attention?

Somewhat, and mostly in boring ways. Sleep helps more than any app. Regular aerobic exercise has fairly good evidence behind it for attention and other control skills in healthy adults. Cutting down how often you switch between tasks helps, because every switch costs a little time and makes the next distraction easier to fall for.

Mindfulness training shows small improvements in some attention measures in studies, though results vary between studies and the effects are modest. Brain training games tend to make you better at the game itself, and the benefit doesn't spread much further.

A practical habit: decide what you're ignoring before you start. Phone in another room, notifications off for an hour. You're taking load off the filter instead of asking it to work harder.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is selective attention?

It's the ability to focus on one source of information, like a voice or a task, while ignoring other things happening at the same time.

### Can you give examples of selective attention?

Following one conversation in a loud room, reading on a busy train, or spotting a friend in a crowd. In each case you pick one thing and let the rest fade.

### How does selective attention work?

The brain turns down information that isn't relevant to your goal and boosts what is. Ignored input isn't fully blocked, which is why your own name can still catch your ear.

### What is the cocktail party effect?

It's the ability to follow one voice among many. It also covers the moment when something important, like your name, breaks through from a conversation you weren't listening to.

### Why is selective attention important?

Almost everything depends on it, from driving to studying. Without it every sound and movement would compete equally for your attention.

### Can selective attention be improved?

To a degree. Good sleep, regular exercise and fewer task switches help most. Brain games mainly improve performance on the games themselves.

### How is selective attention tested?

With tasks such as the Stroop test, dichotic listening, visual search and flanker tasks. Each one times how much a distraction slows you or causes mistakes.

### Does the Stroop test measure selective attention?

Yes. The Stroop test asks you to attend to the ink colour and ignore the word, so the delay on clashing words shows how much the ignored word still got through.
